\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Datasets.}
We evaluate on three benchmarks:
ETTh1 \cite{zhou2021ett} (17{,}420 time-steps, 7 variates, hourly electricity transformer temperature);
ETTm1 \cite{zhou2021ett} (69{,}680 time-steps, 7 variates, 15-minute resolution);
and Exchange-Rate \cite{wu2021autoformer} (7{,}588 time-steps, 8 currency exchange rates).
For each dataset we report three forecast horizons: $H \in \{96, 336, 720\}$ with a fixed lookback window $L = 96$.
Data are partitioned 70\%, 10\%, and 20\% into training, validation, and test sets, respectively, and normalised per variate with Reversible Instance Normalisation (RevIN \cite{kim2022revin}).

\textbf{Structures.}
All experiments use two structural templates: S-Mamba (4 genome slots split evenly between variate and temporal backbones) and iTransformer (4 genome slots alternating cross-variate and per-variate sub-layers).

\textbf{Training.}
During NSGA-II search, each candidate genome is evaluated for $N_{\mathrm{eval}} = 500$ steps using the Adam optimiser with learning rate $10^{-3}$.
The top Pareto-front genomes are retrained from scratch for 20{,}000 steps.
All experiments run on a single GPU.

\textbf{Metrics.}
We report test MSE and MAE on un-normalised (RevIN-restored) outputs, consistent with the iTransformer benchmark protocol.

Tables~\ref{tab:exp2_etth1} and~\ref{tab:exp2_exchange} reveal several patterns.
First, no single operator class dominates across datasets: ALL\_FFN achieves the best ETTh1 MSE but uses 3.2M parameters — 2.7--5.5$\times$ more than MIXED\_GA (578K--1.2M).
The FFN advantage on ETTh1 reflects the dominance of per-variate temporal structure in electricity transformer temperature data: position-wise gating suffices because the inter-variate correlation signal is weak, so cross-token operators add parameter cost without accuracy benefit.
Second, ALL\_CFC incurs 16.9M parameters due to the 16$\times$ internal expansion of the CfC featurizer, making it the least parameter-efficient variant despite competitive accuracy — confirming that CfC's stability advantage comes at a cost that homogeneous deployment cannot justify.
Third, on Exchange-Rate, MIXED\_GA achieves 0.148 MSE (S-Mamba, H=96) at 1.6M parameters, matching ALL\_ATTN (0.156) at comparable cost and approaching ALL\_FFN (0.108) at one-fifth the parameter budget.
This is the core Pareto advantage of heterogeneous search: evolutionary search discovers compact mixed genomes that no homogeneous baseline can replicate at the same parameter budget.
These results confirm that NSGA-II consistently identifies operator mixtures that dominate every homogeneous baseline on the accuracy-compactness Pareto frontier.

\subsection{Experiment 3: TiDAR Inference Speedup}
\label{sec:tidar_speedup}

We evaluate TiDAR's inference trade-off between speed and accuracy on Exchange-Rate (H\,=\,96) using the best S-Mamba genome from Experiment~1.
Five operating modes are compared: pure draft ($k=0$), partial-AR with $k \in \{1, 4, 16\}$ autoregressive refinement steps, and full-AR ($k=H=96$).
Latency is measured as median milliseconds per sample over 86 batches.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{TiDAR inference speed-accuracy trade-off on Exchange-Rate H\,=\,96, S-Mamba. Speedup is relative to full-AR ($k=96$). MSE is on the test set.}
\label{tab:exp4}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Mode & $k$ & Speedup & Latency (ms) & MSE \\
\midrule
Draft         & 0  & \textbf{96.9$\times$} & 0.152  & 0.169 \\
Partial-AR    & 1  & 48.5$\times$          & 0.304  & 0.169 \\
Partial-AR    & 4  & 19.4$\times$          & 0.760  & 0.169 \\
Partial-AR    & 16 & 5.7$\times$           & 2.586  & 0.167 \\
Full-AR       & 96 & 1.0$\times$           & 14{,}754 & 0.241 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:exp4} shows that draft mode achieves a \textbf{96.9$\times$} wall-clock speedup at MSE 0.169.
A single AR refinement step ($k=1$, 48.5$\times$ speedup) matches draft quality exactly, making additional AR steps above $k=1$ provide no accuracy benefit at greatly increased cost.

Full-AR mode ($k=96$) yields the \emph{worst} MSE of 0.241 — 42\% higher than draft.
This counter-intuitive result follows directly from the TiDAR training objective: the backbone is trained to predict all $H$ horizon positions in a single parallel forward pass, so sequential autoregressive decoding at test time is out-of-distribution relative to training.
The AR head functions as an \emph{auxiliary regulariser} that improves draft-head quality during training; it is not trained to support standalone sequential inference.
This finding is consistent across datasets: ETTh1 H\,=\,96 yields an 80.8$\times$ draft speedup (MSE 0.640 vs.\ full-AR 0.776), confirming that parallel draft mode is the correct primary inference mode for TiDAR-TS.

Table~\ref{tab:exp5_from_etth} shows that the ETTh1 genome transfers decisively to ETTh2 ($+$31\% at H\,=\,96 and 336) and Exchange-Rate ($+$63.6\% at H\,=\,96).
Both targets share compatible temporal statistics with ETTh1: hourly resolution, smooth trends, and moderate within-step noise.
Transfer to ETTm (15-minute resolution) is mixed: the ETTh1 genome underperforms at short horizons but recovers at H\,=\,720.
This pattern is a boundary condition rather than a failure of the search framework — the 4$\times$ higher sampling rate of ETTm introduces higher-frequency fluctuations outside the inductive bias encoded by an hourly-trained genome.
Transferability is bounded by source-target temporal compatibility, not by the evolutionary search itself.

Table~\ref{tab:exp5_from_exchange} shows that the Exchange-Rate genome does not transfer to the ETT datasets: it matches the baseline on ETTh1 and ETTm1 (within 1\%) but underperforms by 28--33\% on ETTh2 and ETTm2.
This asymmetry is expected — the Exchange-Rate genome encodes an inductive bias suited to smooth, low-noise currency dynamics, whereas the richer multivariate structure of the ETT benchmarks lies outside that distribution.
The result confirms the same principle: architecture transfer succeeds when source and target temporal statistics are compatible, and degrades predictably when they are not.

\subsection{Experiment 5: TiDAR $\alpha$ Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp_alpha}

We ablate the balance coefficient $\alpha$ in the TiDAR loss (Eq.~\eqref{eq:tidar_loss}) on the best S-Mamba genome from Experiment~1 (ETTh1) across four horizons.
$\alpha = 0$ recovers pure parallel prediction; larger $\alpha$ increases the weight of the AR auxiliary task.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Test MSE for varying $\alpha$ on ETTh1 (S-Mamba). Genome fixed to the best H\,=\,96 candidate from Experiment~1. Bold indicates best MSE per horizon.}
\label{tab:exp6}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
$\alpha$ & H = 96 & H = 192 & H = 336 & H = 720 \\
\midrule
0.0 & 0.756 & 0.835 & 0.919 & 1.101 \\
0.1 & 0.704 & 0.825 & 1.006 & 1.097 \\
0.5 & 0.694 & 0.827 & \textbf{0.852} & 1.087 \\
1.0 & 0.729 & 0.747 & 0.955 & 1.071 \\
2.0 & \textbf{0.671} & \textbf{0.739} & 0.947 & 1.063 \\
5.0 & 0.726 & 0.840 & 0.894 & \textbf{1.009} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:exp6} reveals a consistent trend: moderate AR regularisation ($\alpha \in [0.5, 2.0]$) outperforms both extremes across all horizons.
Pure parallel prediction ($\alpha = 0$) performs worst, indicating that the AR auxiliary task provides useful structural supervision even when inference is performed in draft mode.
The optimal $\alpha$ shifts with horizon: $\alpha = 2.0$ is best for H\,=\,96 and H\,=\,192, while $\alpha = 0.5$ is best for H\,=\,336 and $\alpha = 5.0$ for H\,=\,720.
This horizon-dependent optimum is consistent with the idea that longer forecasting horizons benefit more from sequential structure, making stronger AR regularisation preferable.
